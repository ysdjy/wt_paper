A B S T R A C T
Tool wear is a prominent unfavorable factor that directly affects the machining accuracy and efficiency of CNC 
machine tools. Accurate classification monitoring of tool wear stage is of great significance for high performance 
machining. Existing monitoring methods based on deep learning rarely improves the accuracy of identification 
by establishing non Euclidean structural relationships between multi-sensor signals. A new method for tool wear 
stage classification monitoring based on graph neural network (GNN) is proposed to address these limitations. 
Using convolutional neural network (CNN) and gramian angular summation field (GASF) to extract spatiotem­
poral features from samples. Subsequently, by taking into account both the static and dynamic characteristics of 
temporal data, the time series is transformed into a graph structure using cosine similarity. Following this, a 
graph isomorphism network (GIN) is employed to deeply integrate information from neighboring nodes. The 
dimensionality of the feature matrix and adjacency matrix is progressively reduced through temporal graph 
pooling (TGP). Finally, the classification monitoring of three stages of tool wear is realized. The experimental 
validation was conducted based on the public PHM2010 dataset. The results show that the proposed model 
achieves an average accuracy of 96.51 % in the state recognition task, improved by 0.97 % over the current state- 
of-the-art model. In addition, the recognition accuracy of multi-source information fusion is significantly higher 
than single-signal. The ablation experiments further validate the positive contribution of each module to the 
monitoring performance. In summary, the study provides an efficient and robust solution for tool wear 
monitoring.
1. Introduction
As an important participant in the machining process, cutting tools 
will inevitably suffer from wear due to a variety of reasons, such as 
mechanical action, heat, and chemical factors, which would directly 
affect the surface quality and productivity of parts, and in extreme cases 
may also lead to safety risks [1,2]. Therefore, in order to reasonably 
control the replacement time of tool and improve economic efficiency, it 
is necessary to study the accurate monitoring of tool wear state. Usually, 
the tool wear process throughout the life cycle is generally divided into 
the initial wear stage, normal wear stage and severe wear stage [3,4]. In 
order to ensure the dimensional accuracy and surface roughness of 
finished parts, the severe wear stage should be avoided, and the tool 
should be changed in a timely manner before the arrival of severe stage. 
At the same time, the premature replacement will waste the tool life and 
increase manufacturing costs. During the actual machining process, the 
control of tool wear mainly relies on passive organs or manual experi­
ence, which is very easy to cause misjudgment and omission. In recent 
years, in order to give full play to the tool cutting performance, a ma­
jority of scholars have conducted extensive and in-depth research on 
tool wear state monitoring [5–7].
Different from the tool wear monitoring that quantitatively analyzes 
the amount of wear width value on the flank face, the tool wear stage 
identification aims to establish a nonlinear mapping relationship be­
tween the signal characteristics and wear state, and then quickly classify 
the wear stage. Generally speaking, the tool wear stage recognition has 
been mainly categorized into direct and indirect methods [8]. However, 
due to many limitations in the application of direct methods, most re­
searchers prefer to use indirect methods [9–11]. Indirect method refers 
to the identification of wear state from real-time signals, that is acquired

for constructing a correlation model between the tool wear state and 
monitored data. Compared with the direct method, the indirect method 
can realize continuous and real-time monitoring without affecting the 
machining process [12]. Since signals such as cutting force [13,14], 
acoustic emission [15], vibration [16], current and power [17,18] are 
closely related to the tool wear condition, they are often used for on-line 
monitoring of tool wear state.
Tool wear is a smooth variation process, in the transition region of 
different wear stages, it is difficult to distinguish the tool wear due to the 
blurred boundaries [19]. The training efficiency and recognition accu­
racy of tool wear classification model depend on feature extraction. The 
traditional feature extraction methods involve extracting features from 
the time domain, frequency domain, and time–frequency domain. Sul­
tan Binsaeid et al. [20] extracted different time and frequency domain 
features from sensor signals, such as force, vibration, acoustic emission, 
and spindle power. They used a data acquisition and signal processing 
module and a correlation-based feature selection technique to evaluate 
the importance of these features. Liao et al. [21] employed time domain 
statistics, frequency domain analysis, and wavelet packet decomposition 
to extract features from cutting force signals, and further utilized a ge­
netic algorithm to identify the sensitive features that are highly corre­
lated with the tool wear state. Based on the extracted sensitive features, 
many machine learning methods have been widely applied to monitor 
the tool wear status. The commonly used machine learning models 
include: artificial neural networks (ANN) [22], support vector machines 
(SVM) [23], decision trees (DT) [24], linear discriminant analysis (LDA) 
[25], k-nearest neighbors (KNN) [26], and hidden Markov models 
(HMD) [27].
Despite the success of machine-learning based tool wear state 
recognition models, they are usually built using shallow layers and it is 
difficult to learn deep features from large amounts of data. At the same 
time, the manual extraction process may lead to the loss of more 
important information features in the original signal [28]. With the 
further development of machine learning, deep learning (DL) can 
automatically extract more advanced features from the original signals 
to realize “end-to-end” tool wear state recognition [29–32]. Wang et al. 
[33] proposed a denoising transformer autoencoder for preprocessing of 
tool condition monitoring (TCM) classifiers, which enhances the 
extraction of wear features through a reconstruction task. Tao et al. [34] 
developed an integrated model with long-short-term memory (LSTM) 
network and HMM for tool wear state classification in milling 
machining. Considering that CNN are efficient and flexible in image 
processing, some researchers have converted one-dimensional time-se­
ries signals into two-dimensional images, and then used the channel- 
space attention mechanism to improve the accuracy of feature extrac­
tion for tool wear information, which can reduce the model misclassi­
fication [35]. Wei et al. [36] used continuous wavelet transform to 
convert the force and vibration signals into time–frequency images, and 
realized multi-source information fusion through CNN, and finally 
introduced the channel-space attention module to adaptively adjust the 
weights. Dong et al. [37] converted one-dimensional force signals into 
two-dimensional images based on Markov transition field, and then 
realized tool wear classification using the adaptive-visual-Transformer- 
Kolmogorov-Arnold network (AViTK). However, the aforementioned 
research has primarily focused on sensor feature extraction methods 
within Euclidean spaces. The exploration of potential non-Euclidean 
structural relationships in tool wear monitoring signals remains 
limited, indicating a research gap in this area.
Sensor signals can be modeled as graphs, each feature can be rep­
resented as a node, interconnected through hidden dependencies, 
thereby establishing non-Euclidean structural relationships. GNN cap­
tures the dependencies of graph by passing messages between nodes. In 
recent years, GNN variants such as graph convolutional networks (GCN), 
graph attention networks (GAT), graph recurrent networks (GRN), and 
GIN have demonstrated breakthrough performance in many deep 
learning tasks [38]. For example, GNN have achieved remarkable 
success with graph-structured data such as social networks and medicine 
[39]. In the field of industrial monitoring, Li et al. [40] constructed a 
GNN-based intelligent fault diagnosis and prediction framework, effec­
tively addressing the modeling challenge of irregular graph data in non- 
Euclidean spaces. Wang et al. [41] innovatively proposed a dynamic 
spatiotemporal graph model integrating performance degradation and 
fault propagation. By incorporating unsupervised learning mechanisms, 
it compensates for existing methods’ shortcomings in leveraging engi­
neering prior knowledge. Zhou et al. [42] proposed the Hierarchical 
Temporal-Modal Synchronization Fusion Framework (HTMSFF), 
leveraging GNN technology to effectively enhance tool wear recognition 
accuracy under multimodal monitoring data. Wu et al. [43] designed a 
multi-view fully connected GNN that extracts spatial dependencies 
through local and global graph convolutions, successfully achieving 
equipment remaining life prediction. Zhou et al. [44] developed a tool 
wear recognition model based on multi-scale edge, which utilizes 
labeled GNN for feature extraction on small samples. The proposed 
model outperforms CNN, AlexNet, and ResNet. In addition, Liu et al. 
[45] proposed the TGP method, which combines graph pooling with 
temporal processing, not only reducing the feature dimension but also 
retaining key temporal features. However, none of the studies in the 
field of tool wear monitoring have explored the GIN model, which 
demonstrates superior performance in image classification tasks.
In summary, many scholars have conducted extensive research on 
tool wear state recognition methods, but the author believes that there 
are still the following problems to be solved: 
(1) The existing models perform well on the specific datasets, while 
drop significantly in scenarios with large differences in data 
distribution, indicating that the generalization ability of the 
models still needs to be improved.
(2) Most studies only rely on a single signal for tool wear state 
recognition, failing to fully utilize the complementary nature of 
multi-source information, which limits the further improvement 
in recognition accuracy.
(3) Existing methods primarily focus on Euclidean spaces, rarely 
leveraging the potential non-Euclidean structural relationships 
inherent in temporal data, and insufficiently exploring the 
application of powerful GIN models.
In order to solve the above problems, this paper proposes a new 
method for online identification of tool wear stages based on GIN. The 
main innovations of this paper are as follows: 
(1) A new method for end-to-end tool wear stage classification 
monitoring based on GIN is proposed, and a time–space correla­
tion model of multi-source sequence signals is established. By 
feature extraction on cutting force and vibration signals in time 
and space dimension, multi-source information fusion is realized, 
avoiding the time cost and professional dependence of manual 
feature extraction.
(2) Model the time-series signal as a graph. By comprehensively 
considering the long-term stability and short-term time-varying 
nature of the temporal characteristics, static and dynamic graphs 
are constructed. Leveraging the non-Euclidean structural re­
lationships inherent in the time-series signal enhances the accu­
racy of identifying tool wear states.
(3) The GIN-based TGP method is introduced. This approach in­
tegrates graph pooling and temporal processing techniques to 
achieve feature dimensionality reduction while preserving crit­
ical temporal characteristics, making it particularly suitable for 
handling time-dependent graph data.

monitoring method. Section 3 describes the related experiments and 
collected data. Section 4 performs the experimental validation. Finally,

2. Monitoring methodology
2.1. Overall framework of the monitoring model
The paper proposes a novel method for categorizing and monitoring 
the tool wear stages. The monitoring signals during machining process 
are utilized as input to identify the three distinct types of tool wear 
states. The general framework of the monitoring model is shown in 
Fig. 1. Among them, the time series feature extraction module mines the 
depth dependence between milling force and vibration signals under a 
potential high-dimension space. The spatial feature extraction module 
transforms the time series data into a two-dimensional image by the 
GASF, and further utilizes CNN to extract the embedded spatial features 
of tool wear. Based on the importance of each temporal feature, relevant 
features are extracted from spatial features through an attention 
mechanism and supplemented into the temporal features. The dynamic 
graph generation module takes the overall invariance and local time- 
varying nature of the images into account, and constructs the static 
and dynamic graphs to establish the spatial correlation of different 
features. Finally, the GIN module is used to aggregate the features of 
current node and its neighboring nodes to obtain the tool wear- 
dependent features, and to realize the classification monitoring of tool 
wear stage through the TGP.

2.3. Static and dynamic graphs generation and fusion
In conventional graph theory, the graphs usually have fixed 
connection relationships. However, when monitoring signals acquired 
from tool wear experiments, predefined graph structures are absent. 
Hence, one needs to construct a graph from scratch. In this study, the 
extracted feature variables are designated as nodes with their corre­
sponding feature values are assigned as node attributes, thereby estab­
lishing the spatial correlations. The time-series data, such as cutting 
force and vibration signals, exhibit a combination of stability and vari­
ability by nature. From a holistic perspective, the connection relation­
ships among feature variables remain consistent, representing the static 
information. However, as time progresses, the node attributes continu­
ously evolve, leading to subtle variations in the connection relationships 
at each time interval. This constitutes the dynamic information. 
Consequently, the study considers the stable and variable characteristics 
of series feature simultaneously, constructing the static and dynamic 
graph matrices. Lastly, fusing these two matrices yields an optimal dy­
namic graph, which can reveal the significant and latent spatial corre­
lations among these feature variables.

2.4.2. TGP model
For the graph classification tasks, the graph pooling operation can be 
employed to reduce the number of nodes, extract high-dimensional 
features, and enhance the classification accuracy. However, some con­
ventional pooling methods, such as max pooling, mean pooling, and sum 
pooling, would aggregate feature vectors or matrices into a few features. 
The resulting graph representation vectors tend to be overly smooth, 
neglecting the potential hierarchical structures within the graph and 
failing to learn the hierarchical representations of graph. In this regard, 
the study adopt the TGP method. The method utilizes the temporal 
convolutions to cluster nodes and extract temporal features, followed by 
reconstructing the adjacency matrix using convolutional weights. By 
integrating graph pooling with temporal processing, it not only reduces 
the feature dimensionality but also preserves the key temporal charac­
teristics. This makes it particularly suitable for handling the graph data 
with temporal dependencies.

particularly useful in addressing class imbalance problems. The F1 score 
serves as a harmonic mean of the precision and recall rates, providing a 
combined measure of model robustness, with values closer to one 
indicating a more balanced classification performance.

In the ablation experiments, GNN was first used as the baseline

were performed on the D dataset. The results are shown in Fig. 19. 
Fig. 19(a) shows the test results of the three models on the D1 dataset, 
and Fig. 19(b) shows the test results of the three models on the D3 
dataset. The accuracy reflects the overall performance of the model, and 
the F1 score is the macro average of the three wear stages, compre­
hensively assessing the robustness of the model. The results indicate that 
as the modules are stacked sequentially, the recognition performance for 
tool wear stages improves progressively, demonstrating that the spatio- 
temporal feature extraction module and the TGP based on GIN module 
proposed in the paper effectively enhance model performance.

module on the overall model performance. The modules of temporal

4.5. Performance on NASA datasets
To further validate the generalization capability of this model, 
another publicly available dataset was employed: the NASA Ames tool 
wear dataset. Experiments were conducted on an MC-510 vertical 
machining center at a spindle speed of 826 r/min. A KC710 face milling 
cutter with a blade diameter of 70 mm was used to mill cast iron and 
stainless steel blocks measuring 483 × 178 × 51 mm. Sensors collected 
the following signals: spindle vibration and acoustic emission signals, 
worktable vibration and acoustic emission signals, as well as DC and AC 
signals from the spindle motor. The NASA Ames dataset involved two 
milling runs under each of eight distinct cutting conditions, with rake 
face wear values measured continuously until tool wear became severe 
and the tool became unusable. From the 16 cases, the following three 
were selected for analysis, with specific machining parameters detailed 
in the Table 7.
This study utilizes DC and AC signals from the spindle motor, along 
with acoustic emission signals from both the spindle and worktable. 
Data preprocessing follows Section 3.2, with samples collected from 
stable cutting segments. Due to the limited volume of raw data, stratified 
sampling was not applied to ensure sufficient training samples. Ulti­
mately, 130 samples were collected from each case, totaling 390 sam­
ples. Three wear stages were defined based on wear volume. Three sets 
of cross-validation experiments were designed as Table 8.
The samples were trained and tested on the model, with the corre­
sponding confusion matrix shown in the Fig. 22. As seen in the figure, 
the model performs well on the NASA dataset, correctly identifying most 
samples in the severe wear stage. Minor misclassifications primarily 
occur in the transitional zone between early wear and normal wear, due 
to the lack of clear distinguishing features in this area. Detailed data for 
each wear stage is presented in the Table 9. The accuracy rates for the 
three tools are 99.2 %, 96.9 %, and 98.5 %, respectively, indicating that 
the model correctly classifies most cases with highly stable overall 
performance. Furthermore, the model achieves a good balance between 
precision and recall, particularly excelling in handling the severe wear 
category.
5. Conclusions
The paper proposes an end-to-end tool wear stage recognition 
method based on GNN, addressing the nonlinear mapping problem be­
tween the multi-source sequential signals and tool wear states in both 
temporal and spatial dimensions. The effectiveness of the proposed 
method is validated using publicly available datasets. The main con­
clusions are as follows: 
(1) A spatio-temporal correlation model for multi-source information 
was established, fully utilizing the complementarity of cutting 
force and vibration signals. Through an attention mechanism, it 
achieved the fusion of temporal and spatial features.
(2) For the first time, GIN and TGP are introduced into tool wear 
stage monitoring. By comprehensively considering the long-term 
stability and short-term variability of temporal features, the time- 
series signal is modeled as a graph. Leveraging the inherent non- 
Euclidean structural relationships within the time-series signal 
enhances the accuracy of tool wear state recognition.
(3) Cross-validation using the PHM2010 and NASA datasets 
demonstrated the model’s robust overall performance, high­
lighting the advantages of multi-source information fusion 
alongside strong generalization capabilities and stability.
(4) Experimental validation demonstrates that temporal feature 
extraction, dynamic graph generation, and the TGP module 
significantly enhance performance. Comparative analysis reveals 
that this model outperforms existing methods in both accuracy 
and stability, further validating the significant research value of 
extracting features from the non-Euclidean space of temporal 
signals.
Future research should focus on expanding dataset validation to 
include diverse machining processes and materials, ensuring the 
model’s generalizability and robustness. Additionally, integrating the 
GNN-based approach with advanced techniques like transfer learning or 
reinforcement learning could enhance its adaptability to varying oper­
ational conditions. Finally, improving the model’s explainability is 
crucial for increasing its acceptance in industrial settings.
